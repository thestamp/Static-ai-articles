## Summary
A new New York Times opinion essay argues Republicans have moved away from an older bipartisan disability-rights posture, using George H.W. Bush’s legacy as a benchmark for what policy support once looked like.

## Why this matters now
A current opinion essay in *The New York Times* contends that disabled Americans are being politically sidelined in today’s Republican agenda. Framed against George H.W. Bush’s signing of the Americans with Disabilities Act, the argument centers on whether the party has shifted from institution-building disability policy to a narrower, culture-war posture.

## The policy fault line
The piece’s central claim is not that disability politics were ever conflict-free, but that the baseline changed: where bipartisan disability-rights commitments once translated into durable federal protections, today’s politics produce less predictable support for access, benefits administration, and civil-rights enforcement.

For readers and policymakers, the practical takeaway is to track concrete outputs rather than rhetoric: budget priorities, agency enforcement posture, and legislative language affecting disability services, workplace accommodation, and civil-rights remedies.

## What to watch
1. Federal agency rulemaking and enforcement decisions affecting disability access and discrimination claims.
2. Congressional funding negotiations for disability-linked programs and oversight.
3. Whether either party advances cross-aisle disability legislation with enforceable implementation mechanisms.
